Module 4: Respiratory Disorders in Children

Breathing problems are one of the most common reasons children are seen in the clinic or hospital. A child’s airway is smaller, softer, and more easily blocked than an adult’s, so an illness that is mild in an adult can become an emergency in a child. 👶4.1 How a Child’s Airway Differs from an Adult’sPart 1 · Slides 2–3 + reading

Think of a child’s airway as a smaller, softer, floppier straw. A young child’s airway passages are very small, so even minimal swelling can seriously compromise gas exchange. That’s why the same illness can look much worse in a child than in an adult.

Upper respiratory tract

Lower respiratory tract

More differences
  • Chest wall: more circular, with weak intercostal muscles, leading to fast respiratory fatigue.
  • Head and neck: a short neck and large head make it hard to open the airway using neck flexion, raising the risk of upper airway obstruction.
  • Breathing control: in preterm infants and neonates, breathing control is immature, so they’re at risk for irregular patterns, including apnea, bradypnea, and tachypnea.
  • Oxygen needs: neonates and young infants have higher metabolic demand, so they need more oxygen and are at high risk for hypoxia.
  • A smaller nasopharynx is easily blocked during infection.
  • There are fewer functional muscles in the airway, so it’s less able to compensate for edema, spasm, and trauma, and the large amounts of soft tissue and loosely anchored mucous membranes lining the airway raise the risk of edema and obstruction.

  • Tonsils and adenoids: 📘 The slide image says lymph tissue grows rapidly in early childhood and atrophies after age 12. 📗 The reading says they are enlarged until age 5 to 7, then atrophy.
  • Alveoli: 📘 The slide says alveoli increase in shape and number through the first years of life (age 12). 📗 The reading says they double by 6 months, triple by 12 months, and stop multiplying by 2 or 3 years.
🚨4.2 Respiratory Distress & Its Risk FactorsPart 1 · Slides 4–5 + reading

Respiratory distress is the state in which a child is at risk of a threat to (1) air passing through the respiratory tract and (2) the exchange of O₂ and CO₂ between the lungs and the blood.

Risk factors
Risk factorWhat the sources say
Infectious agentsviruses like RSV, COVID-19, influenza. Most upper respiratory infections spread by contact (touching surfaces, then eyes or nose) and some by droplets.
Age / sizechildren under 2 have the highest per-child rate of URIs; RSV risk is highest in infants under 6 months and premature infants. Smaller airway = less room for swelling.
Resistanceprematurity, Down syndrome, low birth weight, underlying lung or heart disease, daycare, older siblings.
Seasonal variationscolds are more common in fall, winter, and spring, linked to school and holiday gatherings, not cold temperatures. Croup peaks in autumn and early winter. RSV runs from fall to early spring, peaking in the first 2 months of the year.
Excessive or thick secretions / stasis of secretionsMucus that is thick or not moving blocks small airways.
CNS depression or immobilityfrom sedation, surgery, trauma, pain, anxiety, or cognitive impairment.
Extreme high or low humidityAir that is too dry or too humid can irritate the airways.
Sleeping positions of infantsInfants must be placed supine (on the back) for every sleep on a firm, flat surface.
⚠️ Why this matters
In children, respiratory failure is the most common cause of cardiovascular arrest. More serious illness shows tachypnea, increased work of breathing, unexpected lung sounds, hypoxia, retractions, nasal flaring, and changes in level of consciousness that can progress to a minimally responsive state. Without astute assessment and treatment, children can decline quickly.

👀 Look before you touch
The nurse’s first action is to observe the child’s respiratory rate, breathing effort (retractions, nasal flaring, grunting, head bobbing), skin color, and level of consciousness, all before touching the child, because touching often upsets the child and changes the baseline. Letting the child stay in a parent’s lap helps keep them calm. Then move to hands-on: auscultate all lung fields.
📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
Typical respiratory rates by age: newborn/infant 30–60 · toddler 24–40 · preschool 22–34 · school-age 18–30 · adolescent 12–20 (ranges vary a little by source; rate goes down as the child gets older). Tachypnea is usually the first sign of distress; cyanosis is a late sign. Grunting is a short sound at the end of each breath out as the child partly closes the glottis to keep air in the alveoli, a sign of significant distress.
Associated observations
What you’ll see
  • Tachypnea: faster breathing than expected.
  • Retractions: skin pulling in with each breath. A neonate’s intercostal muscles are immature and ineffective, so when they try to use them in distress, retractions appear.
  • Nasal flaring: nostrils widen.
  • Head bobbing: in infants.
  • Color changes: hypoxia often causes cyanosis of the skin and mucous membranes.
What you’ll hear
  • Grunting: a work-of-breathing sign with retractions, flaring, and head bobbing.
  • Cough: barky in croup; productive or dry in other illnesses.
  • Stridor: heard on inspiration with upper airway swelling (croup).
  • Wheezing: when airway passages swell, airflow is restricted and creates a wheeze.
Breath sounds

Normal: inspiration is longer, louder, and higher pitched than expiration. Adventitious (abnormal) sounds: crackles, wheezes, coarse sounds, and stridor.

SoundWhat it suggests
CracklesSecretions (and, in RSV, sloughed epithelial cells) in the airways; heard in bronchitis, RSV, and pneumonia
WheezesSwollen, narrowed airways restricting airflow (asthma, bronchiolitis)
Coarse sounds / rhonchiLung congestion (for example, in cystic fibrosis)
StridorInspiratory sound from upper airway swelling (croup, epiglottitis, foreign body)
Prolonged expiratory phaseInflammation in the bronchi and bronchioles (bronchitis, RSV)
Where retractions happen

Five locations: suprasternal (above the breastbone), supraclavicular (above the collarbones), intercostal (between the ribs), substernal (below the breastbone), and subcostal (below the ribs).

📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
Retractions climb the ladder: the higher the retraction (up toward the neck), the worse the distress. Mild = subcostal/substernal → moderate = intercostal → severe = suprasternal/supraclavicular.

When to give it
Most resources call for supplemental oxygen when a child’s saturation is below 95% on room air with signs of distress (retractions, flaring, grunting). The expected SpO₂ range is 95% to 100%; it may be lower in children with chronic respiratory illness or cyanotic heart disease, so follow the provider’s parameters.
❓ Is there such a thing as too much oxygen?
Oxygen is titrated as prescribed while monitoring pulse oximetry. Oxygen also causes insensible fluid loss and hypothermia in vulnerable children, so provide humidified, warmed oxygen when possible.
📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
Oxygen is a drug: give the amount ordered and titrate to the target SpO₂. Too much can cause oxygen toxicity and, in premature infants, eye damage (retinopathy of prematurity).
Aerosol / nebulizer therapy

Medications: bronchodilators (albuterol), steroids, anticholinergics, and antibiotic nebs.

✅ Assessment of effectiveness is key
Check SpO₂, respiratory rate, and breath sounds before and after. A drop in SpO₂ suggests hypoxia; a rising respiratory rate with diminished breath sounds suggests bronchoconstriction. If symptoms don’t improve after the medication, the child may need more treatment, so notify the provider.

🫶4.5 Chest PT, Artificial Ventilation & Tracheostomy CarePart 1 · Slides 13–16 + reading
Chest PT / postural drainage
  • Purpose: remove excessive fluid or mucus.
  • Position with gravity so each lung segment drains.
  • Percussion with a cupped hand, or a device for infant percussion.
  • Every 4–6 hours, in 20–30 minute intervals.
  • Deep breathing: be creative. Try blowing bubbles or pinwheels for young children who can’t use an incentive spirometer.
🍽️ Timing around meals
Airway clearance therapy (which includes chest physiotherapy with postural drainage) is ideally done 2 to 4 times daily and is avoided right before or right after meals.
Artificial ventilation
  • Indications: progressive hypoxia from inadequate ventilation, excessive work of breathing, or inadequate respiratory effort.
  • Bag and mask at the bedside.
  • Respiratory assessment.
  • Mouth care.
Tracheostomy tubes

A tracheostomy is a surgical opening of the trachea below the 2nd and 4th tracheal ring. Indications: trauma, tumors, or other abnormalities of the airway. A tracheostomy bypasses the nose and mouth.

🧰 What equipment is necessary?
Non-sterile and sterile gloves, a tracheostomy kit, sterile water or 0.9% sodium chloride, sterile 4×4 gauze, a disposable inner cannula, a pulse oximeter, and new ties or a tube holder. Suction must be readily available at all times, plus the bag and mask.
🧼 Care of the tracheostomy
  • Hyperoxygenate with 100% oxygen for at least 30 seconds (or 5–6 deep breaths) before care.
  • Changing ties: if an assistant is available, they hold the tube in place while the old ties are cut. With no assistant, leave the old ties on until the new ones are secured.
  • Ties go around the neck twice and are knotted with room for fingers underneath (see “Sources differ”).
📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
Other indications: long-term ventilation, upper airway obstruction (congenital narrowing, tumors, severe swelling), and trouble clearing secretions. Pediatric trach tubes are usually uncuffed (the narrow airway seals itself). Emergency equipment at the bedside: a spare trach tube in the same size and one size smaller, the obturator (guide for reinsertion), suction with correctly sized catheters, oxygen and a bag-valve device, scissors, extra ties, saline, and gauze.
Suctioning a tracheostomy
  • When: only when needed, not on a set schedule. Signs a child needs suctioning: visible secretions, increased difficulty breathing, a mucus plug, wet or noisy breathing, color change.
  • Catheter size should be closely matched to the trach tube size (infant 5–6 Fr, child 6–8 Fr, adolescent 8–10 Fr).
  • Use sterile technique for open suctioning. Hyperoxygenate first if indicated.
  • Insert at the end of inspiration to the predetermined length, with no suction while inserting: the suction port open during insertion and closed (covered) during withdrawal, which is when suction is applied.
  • Closed tracheal suctioning is another method, where the catheter stays inside a sleeve attached to the tube.
  • Apply suction while withdrawing and rotating, for 10 seconds or less (to limit hypoxemia and trauma). Rest 30–60 seconds between attempts, using the bag-valve-mask or deep breaths if needed.
  • Going too deep can cause bradycardia and cardiac arrhythmias.
📚 Sources differ
  • How deep to suction: 📘 Slide 16 figure says the catheter goes just slightly beyond the end of the tracheostomy tube. 📗 The reading’s skill step says it should not extend beyond the trach tube by more than 0.5 cm, while another line in the same skill says it should not extend beyond the end of the tube.
  • Tie tightness: 📘 Slide 16 figure: ties are snug but allow one finger. 📗 Reading: leave room for one or two fingers.

🦭4.6 Croup (Acute Laryngotracheobronchitis) & the Croup SyndromesPart 2 · Slides 2–3, 7 + reading

Acute laryngotracheobronchitis (LTB, croup) is inflammation and obstruction of the upper airway (larynx and trachea) caused by a virus. It is usually preceded by a URI.

🦠 Cause & risk
Viruses such as RSV, influenza A and B, and most often parainfluenza. Risk factors: ages 6 months to 3 years, more often males, family history, autumn and early winter. Smoke exposure at home causes more severe croup. Spasmodic croup is more common in children with allergies, and symptoms occur at night.
🔊 What you’d expect
1–2 days of coryza, nasal discharge, and congestion → around day 3: fever, hoarseness, and a barky cough, with inspiratory stridor as swelling increases. As it worsens: faster breathing and a longer inspiratory phase, then signs of respiratory distress. The table adds restlessness, irritability, low-grade fever, and a nontoxic appearance. Croup improves with humidified air.
Therapeutic management and nursing care
  • Goals: maintain the airway and provide adequate respiratory exchange.
  • Interventions: cool mist, nebulized racemic epinephrine, and an oral steroid (dexamethasone).
  • Dexamethasone: glucocorticoid; give in the morning to avoid insomnia, with food to avoid stomach upset. Side effects: insomnia, acne, indigestion, increased appetite, fluid retention, agitation.
  • At home: humidified air and good hydration. Croup is usually self-limiting, and because it’s usually viral, antibiotics usually aren’t needed.
  • In the hospital: isolate from other children, and keep the child comfortable and calm, because upset can compromise the airway more. Position of comfort: raise the head of the bed and allow a parent’s lap.

💡 Telling them apart
Croup = slow onset, barky cough, nontoxic. Epiglottitis = rapid onset, drooling, toxic. In epiglottitis cough is often absent. Tracheitis = looks like croup but doesn’t respond to croup treatment and has pus. Note that racemic epinephrine gives only short-term improvement.
🚑4.7 Acute EpiglottitisPart 2 · Slides 4–6 + reading

Acute epiglottitis is a serious obstructive, inflammatory process of the epiglottis, with potential for complete respiratory obstruction. Why: the epiglottis normally covers the trachea when you swallow; when it’s inflamed, it can completely cover the trachea, causing respiratory failure and cardiopulmonary arrest if not treated immediately.

🦠 Etiology
Follows a minor URI or sore throat. Usually bacterial (influenza B or other bacteria), most commonly Haemophilus influenzae type B (Hib). Since the Hib vaccine, epiglottitis in children has declined significantly, but unvaccinated children remain at risk. Non-infectious causes: smoke, chemicals, hot foods or drinks, vaping.
🫢 The 4 Ds
Dyspnea · Dysphagia · Dysphonia · Drooling. Children under 5 with Hib epiglottitis may be in severe distress, won’t lie supine, and prefer the tripod (orthopnea) position. Unlike croup, cough is often absent and drooling is excessive.
Tripod position

The child sits leaning forward, with mouth open and neck and chin extended, while the swollen epiglottis obstructs the airway.

🚫 Do not inspect the throat
The nurse should not inspect the throat directly; doing so could cause laryngeal spasm and completely block the airway. Labs are often not drawn for fear that painful procedures will cause more irritation. Airway management tools must stay with the child during diagnosis. Diagnosis is usually by clinical presentation, sometimes with an x-ray of the airway (lateral neck film).
Therapeutic management and care
Goals & interventions
Goals: maintain the airway; assist with intubation.
Interventions: administer meds; administer oxygen as prescribed; suction as needed; monitor for distress; relieve fear, anxiety, and stress.
In the hospital
A calming environment to avoid triggering laryngeal spasm; continuous parent presence; avoid any treatment that causes crying (crying increases swelling). Keep tracheostomy supplies at the bedside; they go with the child when leaving the room, and the child never goes for testing without a nurse.
👪 Teaching
Parents should understand that epiglottitis is an emergency and seek help immediately if they suspect it. Parents who choose not to vaccinate should be educated about infections like epiglottitis and how severe they can be.
👅4.8 Pharyngitis, Tonsillitis & Post-Tonsillectomy CarePart 2 · Slides 8–9 + reading

Etiology: viruses, or group A beta-hemolytic strep (Streptococcus pyogenes). Red, swollen tonsils with white exudate and swollen neck glands.

❓ What is it?
Pharyngitis is inflammation of the pharynx (throat). In strep pharyngitis, group A strep invades the lining of the pharynx, causing inflammation and secretions; strep is the most common bacterial cause in children and accounts for about a third of throat infections. Tonsillitis is inflammation of the tonsils, which are lymphatic tissue at the back of the throat that trap organisms and help clear infection.
👀 What would you expect to see? (strep)
A severe sore throat that starts abruptly, often without nasal drainage, with fever. The throat is red with petechiae and white patchy exudate. A strawberry tongue and a rough, red “sandpaper” rash on the trunk (scarlatina rash) may appear, and neck lymph nodes are more swollen than with a viral infection. Viral causes more often bring cough and runny nose. In tonsillitis, difficulty swallowing is concerning because swelling can threaten the airway.
🧪 Diagnosis
A throat swab (rapid strep test). Rapid tests can give false negatives, so if it’s negative but strep is still suspected, the swab is sent for culture.
What you’d expect
Tonsillitis is most common at ages 5 to 15 (rare under 2), in winter and early spring. Strep pharyngitis findings: a bright red pharynx with small red-purple dots and enlarged tonsillar tissue with white spots. Complications of GAS: rheumatic fever and heart disease, glomerulonephritis, and peritonsillar abscess.
Treatment
Viral: self-limiting. Bacterial: antibiotics, usually amoxicillin (penicillin family; can be given once a day). A one-time IM penicillin may be used if adherence is a concern. Allergic: cephalosporin, macrolide, or clindamycin. Supportive care: cold liquids and over-the-counter pain medicine (acetaminophen or ibuprofen). Give the full course of antibiotics even when the child feels better. Most children return to baseline after 1–2 days of antibiotics, and the child may return to school in 24 hours if there’s no fever.
🥤 Getting a sore-throated child to drink
Keeping the child comfortable and hydrated is often the hardest part. Be creative: fun drinking devices (like silly straws), making drinking a game, and offering hydration choices. Assess hydration and treat pain with a developmentally appropriate pain scale. (This is for tonsillitis, not post-tonsillectomy, not post-tonsillectomy care.)
Care of the child with a tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy
  • Rarely performed under age 3 because of the risk for bleeding and tonsillar regrowth.
  • Airway: positioning.
  • Bleeding: observe for frequent swallowing (a sign the child is swallowing blood); prevent recurrent bleeding; maintain a quiet environment and minimize agitation and crying.
  • Diet.
  • Pain: the FACES scale for a 3-year-old after tonsillectomy (the numeric scale is for children 5 and older), because pain is subjective even for a 3-year-old. Vital sign changes and temperature are not reliable pain measures.
  • Post-operative hemorrhage: unusual but serious.
📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
  • Prevent bleeding: minimize crying, coughing, throat clearing, nose blowing, and gargling. No straws. No red or brown foods or drinks (they look like blood).
  • Diet: start with cool clear liquids (ice chips, water, apple juice, non-red popsicles), then soft, bland foods. Avoid citrus (stings), hot, spicy, and rough foods (chips, toast). Avoid milk products at first (they coat the throat and make the child clear it).
  • Pain: analgesics on a schedule (acetaminophen; avoid aspirin); an ice collar may help.
  • Hemorrhage timing: most likely in the first 24 hours or around days 5–10, when the scabs slough off. Teach parents to call or return for any bright red bleeding.

A child having surgery for recurrent tonsillitis benefits from a child life specialist and atraumatic care techniques.

👂4.9 Otitis Media (OM)Part 2 · Slides 10–11 + reading

Otitis media is an infection of the middle ear. Etiology: viral or bacterial. A normal eardrum with a red, inflamed one.

Related facts
Children with cleft palate have a higher risk of ear infections because of eustachian tube placement and function problems, and frequent ear infections and fluid buildup can cause temporary or permanent hearing loss if untreated. Children with chronic ear infections may have tubes placed in both ears, and amoxicillin is commonly used to treat ear infections.
Therapeutic management and care
ApproachDetails
Watch and waitObservation before treating.
Comfort measuresAcetaminophen and ibuprofen.
AntibioticsAmoxicillin: take the entire course at the same time daily; shake suspensions; refrigeration preferred; the child should feel better within 24–48 hours; side effects include nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and allergic reactions.
SurgeryTube placement for chronic infections.
📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
  • Risk factors: daycare, secondhand smoke, bottle feeding while lying flat (“bottle propping”), pacifier use, recent cold, cleft palate, allergies, family history. Breastfeeding is protective. Children’s eustachian tubes are shorter, wider, and more horizontal, so fluid drains poorly.
  • What you’d expect: infants tug or pull at the ear, roll the head, are fussy, won’t lie down, and may refuse to suck; fever, irritability, poor sleep, decreased hearing. Otoscope: a red, bulging eardrum with no visible landmarks or light reflex.
  • Watch and wait: for mild cases in otherwise healthy children, the provider may observe for 48–72 hours before starting antibiotics, with a clear follow-up plan.
  • Prevention: never prop the bottle, avoid smoke exposure, keep vaccines current, finish all antibiotic doses, and follow up on hearing and speech with recurrent infections.

🦠4.10 The Reactive Lower Airway, Bronchiolitis & RSVPart 3 · Slides 1–4 + reading

The lower airways (bronchi and bronchioles) are the “reactive” part of the respiratory tract. Their cartilage support isn’t fully developed until adolescence, so they constrict easily.

Bronchiolitis is inflammation and obstruction of the small airways (bronchioles). Etiology: follows a URI; caused by RSV. What happens inside: necrosis and loss of the epithelium, mucus buildup, inflamed tissue, and smooth muscle tightening. The result is some alveoli over-inflated with trapped air and some collapsed.

🦠 RSV facts
Bronchiolitis is seen most often in children under 2. RSV runs fall to early spring, peaking in the first 2 months of the year. Highest risk: infants under 6 months, premature infants, Down syndrome, low birth weight, lung or heart disease, daycare, older siblings. RSV spreads by droplets and by contact (touch).
Signs and symptoms by stage

Observe first (rate, work of breathing, color, LOC), then auscultate. Expect a prolonged expiratory phase and crackles from secretions and sloughed cells; wheezing may be heard with or without a stethoscope.

Therapeutic management and care
🛏️ Supportive care
  • Contact and droplet precautions throughout hospitalization.
  • Monitoring oxygenation. Give humidified, warmed oxygen when possible and titrate as prescribed.
  • Monitoring IV or NG fluids. Watch for fluid overload (lung sounds, edema), strict I&O, daily weights.
  • Monitoring for fever; administering prescribed medications.
  • Bulb suctioning to clear infant nasal passages; suction nares before feeding and teach parents.
  • The highest safety priority for a hospitalized child with RSV is airway management.
🛡️ Prevention of RSV
Nirsevimab (and clesrovimab) are monoclonal antibodies given to infants entering their first RSV season and select high-risk children 8 to 19 months entering their second season. Give the first injection before RSV season starts and to teach signs of anaphylaxis, injection-site reaction, and RSV infection.
👪 Family support & education
Teach age-appropriate hydration, bulb suctioning to clear secretions, fever management, and how to recognize worsening. Limit bulb suctioning to four times per day to reduce trauma to the nares.
🌪️4.11 Asthma: Triggers, Zones & MedicationsPart 3 · Slides 5–10 + reading

Asthma is a chronic, reversible, inflammatory disorder of the airways that causes episodic airway obstruction and hyperresponsiveness to many stimuli. It results from triggers and consists of bronchoconstriction and inflammation. Expiratory airflow decreases, trapping gas in the airways and causing alveolar hyperinflation. The exact cause isn’t known; environmental factors and family history are thought to play a role.

Clinical manifestations
🟡 Initial symptoms
Coughing, wheezing, mild shortness of breath → “yellow zone” on the peak flow meter (Asthma Action Plan).
🔴 Progressive symptoms
Marked respiratory distress, changes in position (sitting forward, tripod), “red zone” on the peak flow meter or action plan. Status asthmaticus = EMERGENCY.
Diagnosis
  • Clinical signs and history. No lab test directly diagnoses asthma; physical exam, history, and pulmonary function tests do. A baseline chest x-ray may show overinflation.
  • Peak expiratory flow: how much air the child can exhale (the amount exhaled in one second). Used to determine optimal peak flows, usually in primary care, and to set the child’s green, yellow, and red zones and teach families early signs.
  • Blood gases and pulse oximetry.
  • RAST testing to assess for allergens and triggers. Also allergy testing, bronchoprovocation testing, and exercise testing.

🧬4.12 Cystic Fibrosis (CF)Part 3 · Slides 11–18 + reading

What is it? A chronic, autosomal recessive, inherited disorder of the exocrine glands that affects multiple organ systems. Etiology: a CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) gene mutation that causes abnormal sodium and water transport. With a family history, prenatal DNA testing can be done.

🧬 Inheritance
When both parents are carriers, each child has a 25% chance of CF, a 50% chance of being a carrier, and a 25% chance of being unaffected. A child will develop CF if both parents have CF. Partners can be tested (blood or saliva) to learn whether they’re carriers.
Pathophysiology

Symptoms come from mechanical obstruction caused by thick mucus. Instead of a thin, freely flowing secretion, the mucous glands produce a thick, heavy mucoprotein that accumulates and dilates them. The mucus lets organisms multiply in the lungs (infection), irritates and scars lung tissue, and impairs gas exchange; it blocks pancreatic enzymes (poor digestion and absorption, and diabetes); fertility problems are common. More sodium and chloride are lost in sweat, which can lead to hyponatremia.

Sinuses: sinusitisLungs: thick mucus, bacterial infection, widened airwaysSkin: salty sweatLiver: blocked biliary ductsPancreas: blocked ductsIntestines: can’t fully absorb nutrientsReproductive complications
Diagnostic evaluation
  • Family history; prenatal DNA testing; newborn screening in all 50 states.
  • Sweat chloride test: the most reliable diagnostic procedure. Sodium and chloride in sweat are 2–5 times higher than controls, because they can’t be reabsorbed in the sweat ducts.
  • Genetic testing identifies the specific mutation; a nutritional panel shows deficiency of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K); CBC may show anemia; chest x-ray may show emphysema, pneumonia, or atelectasis.
Respiratory manifestations
May show at birth or take years
Dyspnea, dry non-productive cough, wheezing, atelectasis and obstructive emphysema from mucus obstruction, chronic sinusitis, bronchitis, bronchopneumonia. Hemoptysis: potentially life-threatening. Also rhonchi from lung congestion, nasal congestion, and sinus infections.
As disease progresses
Signs of chronic CO₂ retention: barrel-shaped chest, clubbing of fingers, cyanosis. Less O₂/CO₂ exchange → hypoxia, hypercapnia, acidosis. Compressed pulmonary blood vessels and worsening lung function → pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, respiratory failure, and death.
CF respiratory management

Goal: good pulmonary hygiene with a daily routine of chest PT (CPT).

  • Airway clearance therapies (ACTs): CPT and postural drainage; vests that vibrate the chest to loosen secretions.
  • Promote physical exercise.
  • Medications: bronchodilators; mucolytics such as dornase alfa (thins sputum; given twice a day; assess ability to cough up sputum); antibiotics as needed; CFTR modulators such as elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (Trikafta), which make the faulty CFTR protein work better (only for certain mutations; give with a fat-containing meal or snack; avoid grapefruit; eye exams for cataracts; liver blood tests).
  • Humidified oxygen as needed.
  • sodium chloride supplements may be prescribed to replace salt lost in sweat in the summer.
📚 Sources differ: how often to do airway clearance
📘 Slide 15: CPT usually at least 2 times per day. 📗 Reading: ACT is ideally done 2 to 4 times daily and avoided right before or after meals.
GI manifestations & GI management
🍼 What you’d see
  • Meconium ileus at birth (not passing meconium can be a sign of CF).
  • Weight loss despite increased appetite.
  • Malnourishment and vitamin deficiency.
  • Malabsorption with chronic diarrhea and large, frothy, foul-smelling stools (steatorrhea).
  • Long-term GI complications. Also chronic constipation, abdominal pain, low BMI, growth restriction, and delayed puberty.
🥑 GI management
  • Pancreatic enzymes. Enzymes and vitamins given within 30 minutes of eating meals and snacks; capsules can be opened and sprinkled on an acidic food like applesauce; don’t chew or crush the contents.
  • High-protein, high-calorie diet. Children with CF need up to 150% of the recommended daily allowances, and that increasing fiber isn’t indicated (more bulk, more malabsorption).
  • Prevention and early management of intestinal obstruction; reduction of rectal prolapse; regular nutritional monitoring.
  • Megestrol may be prescribed to increase appetite (contraception needed for adolescents).
Therapeutic management

Comprehensive assessment of all affected systems, with primary focus on pulmonary and GI. Interventions focus on hospital care, home care, family support, and transition to adulthood. Routines (ACT twice a day, enzymes with meals, hand washing) give young children comfort and predictability; regular follow-up includes sputum samples, PFTs every 3 months, blood tests, and imaging; outcomes have improved so much that many people with CF live well into adulthood.

🧩4.13 Foreign Body AspirationPart 3 · Slides 19–22 + reading

What’s most common? Anything small enough to swallow is a risk. Common objects: button batteries, seeds, nuts, hot dogs, grapes, and candy. Small children explore with their mouths; aspiration can also happen when a child inhales while crying or laughing, or with neurological disease or loss of consciousness.

Presentation depends on the object and its location:

⬆️ Upper airway (partial or complete obstruction)
Cough, wheeze, stridor, dyspnea, cyanosis, voice changes, retractions.
⬇️ Lower airway
One-sided wheezing, diminished breath sounds on one side, asymmetry of the chest. (Recall from 4.1: the right bronchus is shorter, wider, and more vertical.)
Mechanisms of obstruction
DegreeWhat happens to airflow
First-degreeObstruction allows air to pass in both directions.
Second-degreeAir moves past the obstruction in one direction only. Air passages enlarge during inspiration and diminish during expiration.
CompleteAir can’t move in either direction; the object and swollen mucosa block the passage.

Diagnosis: a coin can be seen on front and side chest x-rays. Providers and radiologists use x-rays and CT scans to look for swallowed or aspirated objects, and a pulmonologist may perform a bronchoscopy.

Treatment & nursing care
🆘 In an emergency
Heimlich maneuver: back blows (with chest thrusts) for infants and abdominal thrusts for toddlers and older children. Once the airway is clear, stay with the child until emergency care is provided and place them side-lying. Bronchoscopy can remove the object.
🛡️ Education for prevention is key
Cut foods (especially round or cylindrical ones) into thin slices or small pieces; remove seeds and pits; children eat sitting upright, calm, supervised, taking small bites (remind older kids not to rush); hold infants or use a highchair and don’t prop bottles; no toys with small parts; follow toy age labels; secure battery compartments; lock cabinets; keep detergent pods and small objects out of reach. Hospital hazards include tape, oral-care sponges, IV port caps, and food.
📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
No nuts or popcorn under age 4; cut grapes and hot dogs lengthwise and then smaller; use the toilet-paper-roll test for toy size (anything that fits through is a choking hazard); keep coins and balloons out of reach; parents can learn infant and child CPR.

After a choking event, some children develop phagophobia (fear of swallowing), which can reduce intake and cause dehydration and weight loss; they need psychological and nutritional support.

🔄5.1 From Fetal to Newborn CirculationSlide 4

Before birth, blood bypasses the fluid-filled lungs through two fetal shunts: the foramen ovale (between the atria) and the ductus arteriosus (connecting the pulmonary artery and aorta).

What changes at birth
  1. First breath: the lungs expand → pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) decreases.
  2. Increased pulmonary blood flow returns more blood to the left atrium.
  3. Clamping the umbilical cord removes the low-resistance placenta → systemic vascular resistance (SVR) and systemic blood pressure increase.
  4. Blood flow shifts from the fetal shunts to pulmonary circulation. Blood flows from higher pressure (left side) to lower pressure (right side), following the path of least resistance.
  5. Normally the PDA and PFO close within 24–48 hours after birth.
🧭 The rule that explains every shunt
Blood follows the path of least resistance, from high pressure to low pressure. After birth the left side is the high-pressure side, so a hole between the two sides shunts blood left → right, sending extra oxygenated blood back to the lungs.
💊 Keeping the ductus open: prostaglandin E₁ (alprostadil)
Some newborns need the ductus kept open until surgery (critical coarctation, transposition). Alprostadil maintains patency of the ductus arteriosus; adverse effects listed are hypotension (in neonates under 2 kg) and syncope. Teach the family it keeps the ductus open until surgical repair.

Cyanosis can also come from a defect that lets oxygenated and deoxygenated blood mix in the heart chambers or great arteries.

Cardiovascular screening
👶 Newborn pulse-ox screen for CHD
Done when the infant is at least 24 hours old, comparing SpO₂ in the right hand (preductal) and the foot (postductal). Failed if either reading is under 90% at any time, or if after one repeat test 1 hour later it’s still under 95% or the two differ by more than 3%. A failed screen means more testing is needed; it doesn’t prove a defect.
📏 Other screening
Blood pressure at annual well-child visits starting at age 3 for children without risk factors; at every visit (well and ill) for children with risk factors. Dyslipidemia screening for all children 9 to 11 years. Most CHD can be found prenatally by fetal ultrasound and echocardiography. Heart failure, endocarditis, and Kawasaki disease have no standardized screening.
Infective endocarditis

Children with CHD are at risk for infective endocarditis, so prophylactic antibiotics are recommended before dental procedures. 50–70% of infective endocarditis is seen in children with CHD; common organisms are Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes. Prevention includes oral hygiene, dental visits at least every 6 months, and prophylactic antibiotics for high-risk clients having invasive procedures.

👢5.4 Tetralogy of Fallot, Coarctation of the Aorta & TranspositionSlides 8–10 + reading
Tetralogy of Fallot (TOF): the most common cyanotic defect

Four abnormalities: VSD, pulmonary stenosis, overriding aorta, right ventricular hypertrophy.

🧠 “PROVe” it’s TOF
Pulmonary stenosis · Right ventricular hypertrophy · Overriding aorta · VSD
😵 Tet spells
sudden hypercyanosis, hyperpnea, agitation; may progress to syncope. Spells need prompt action to prevent seizure or cardiac arrest. First step: place the infant in a knee-to-chest position. Keep the environment calm; give oxygen and morphine as prescribed (watch for respiratory depression, sedation, hypotension, bradycardia).
🧎 Why squatting / knees-to-chest helps
↑ SVR → ↓ R→L shunt → ↑ oxygenation. The position increases afterload and decreases the amount of deoxygenated blood coming back from the legs. Older children squat on their own.

Other findings: clubbing of fingers and toes (chronic hypoxemia); a harsh systolic murmur at the left upper sternal border (from the pulmonary stenosis). Chest x-ray: boot-shaped heart; echocardiogram confirms all four defects. Treatment: surgical repair. Elective surgery at 2 to 6 months (sooner if stenosis is severe) repairs the VSD and pulmonary valve and improves RV outflow.

Coarctation of the aorta (CoA)

A narrowing just distal to the left subclavian artery, near the ductus arteriosus. 50–80% also have a bicuspid aortic valve. Critical neonatal CoA is ductus-dependent: the infant can collapse when the ductus closes. In utero there’s no problem, but as the PDA and foramen ovale close, the left ventricle has to work harder.

💪 Arms high, legs low
BP and pulses higher in the upper extremities, lower in the lower extremities. Turner syndrome (45,X): 30–35% have CoA. Males 2:1. Older children: headaches, epistaxis, leg fatigue. Severe neonatal CoA can show pallor, irritability, dyspnea, diaphoresis, absent femoral pulses, a murmur, and hepatomegaly; milder cases may have no symptoms, especially while the PDA is open.
🛠️ Diagnosis & treatment
Echocardiogram. Treatment: prostaglandin E₁ and surgery. Alprostadil keeps the ductus open and lowers left ventricular pressure until surgery; balloon angioplasty via cardiac catheterization is another option. Nursing: focused cardiac assessments, neurovascular status, vital signs, watching for hemodynamic distress.
Transposition of the great arteries (TGA)

The aorta arises from the right ventricle (instead of the left) and the pulmonary artery from the left ventricle (instead of the right). The great vessels are switched, creating a parallel circuit, not a series: oxygen-poor blood keeps circling the body and oxygen-rich blood keeps circling the lungs.

🔵 Hallmark
Profound cyanosis without improvement with O₂. X-ray: “egg on a string.” Echocardiogram confirms.
🛠️ Treatment sequence
(1) Prostaglandin E₁ keeps the PDA open to allow mixing → (2) balloon atrial septostomy enlarges the atrial opening if mixing is still inadequate → (3) definitive repair with the arterial switch operation (Jatene procedure) at 1–2 weeks of life.
👪 Family-centered care for CHD
Refer to a child life specialist (creative play to reduce anxiety) and to a social worker and parent support groups for emotional, financial, and practical support; palliative care can help manage symptoms. Soothe crying infants quickly: prolonged crying increases the heart’s workload. After heart surgery: manage pain before activity or dressing changes; don’t lift the child by the arms or armpits, don’t let them pull up with their arms or raise arms overhead, and no lifting over 2.27 kg (5 lb). Pre- and post-op: deep breathing and coughing (incentive spirometer, or bubbles and pinwheels for young children), therapeutic play, guided imagery, music.

💉 Vaccines after IVIG

💔5.7 Pediatric Heart FailureSlides 17–21 + reading

Heart failure (HF) is a syndrome in which the heart can’t deliver enough cardiac output to meet the body’s metabolic demands. In adults, coronary artery disease is the most common cause; in children, HF most often comes from congenital defects with large left-to-right shunts (VSD, ASD, PDA) or cardiomyopathy. The ideal treatment for HF caused by CHD is to correct the defect.

Compensatory mechanisms

These are initially protective, eventually harmful:

  • Sympathetic nervous system activation: tachycardia, stronger contractions, peripheral vasoconstriction. This eventually leads to myocardial toxicity and dysfunction.
  • RAAS activation: sodium and water retention and volume expansion → worsens ventricular overload. Angiotensin and aldosterone also promote cardiac fibrosis.
  • Ventricular hypertrophy and dilation: remodeling to maintain output.

  • Weight gain over 50 g/day in an infant with HF = notify the provider (fluid retention and decompensation).
📞 Vomiting after digoxin
If a parent calls because the infant vomited right after a digoxin dose, the nurse’s first action is to tell the parent not to give a repeat dose. The greatest risk is digoxin toxicity; the nurse follows up, and if vomiting continues, notifies the provider, because vomiting is a possible sign of toxicity.

🆘5.9 Pediatric Shock & AnaphylaxisBlueprint · GI reading + drug cards
Hypovolemic shock

Severe dehydration can lead to hypovolemic shock, cell dysfunction, and organ failure. Infants and young children can’t change their stroke volume to raise cardiac output, so they compensate with tachycardia, tachypnea, and shunting blood to vital organs.

⏳ Early signs
Tachycardia, tachypnea, prolonged capillary refill, and color changes in the extremities and skin (cool or mottled).
📉 Late signs
Weak or absent peripheral pulses, low blood pressure, and altered mental status. Blood pressure is a late sign of shock in children.
🚑 Treatment
For severe dehydration or hypovolemia: isotonic 0.9% sodium chloride as a rapid IV bolus, typically 10–20 mL/kg within 20 minutes, repeated if perfusion doesn’t improve. Avoid hypotonic boluses. Give a smaller initial bolus to children with heart failure, chronic cardiac disease, or risk of fluid overload to avoid pulmonary congestion. IV fluids (not ORS) are used when the child is unstable, persistently vomiting, or shows signs of shock.
📚 Sources differ: bolus size
📘 Your GI slides: 20 mL/kg isotonic NS or LR STAT for severe dehydration with hypotension. 📗 Your GI reading: 10 to 20 mL/kg of 0.9% NaCl within 20 minutes.
Signs of anaphylaxis

Anaphylaxis signs include rash, itching, hives, wheezing, dyspnea, dysphagia, angioedema, and skin blistering and peeling. After a nirsevimab injection, teach families the signs of anaphylaxis.

📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)
  • Epinephrine first, epinephrine fast: IM epinephrine into the mid-outer thigh is the first and most important drug; it opens the airway and raises BP, and can be repeated every 5–15 minutes.
  • Then call for help, position the child (lying with legs raised if dizzy; sitting up if struggling to breathe), give oxygen, and give IV fluids for hypotension. Antihistamines and steroids are add-ons, not substitutes. Watch for a biphasic (second-wave) reaction for several hours.
  • Family teaching: carry two auto-injectors at all times; inject through clothing if needed; always go to the ED after use; a written school allergy action plan; medical alert ID; read food labels.

💧6.1 Pediatric DehydrationGI · Slides 3–4 + reading

Dehydration occurs when fluid output exceeds intake, causing a net loss of total body water. Most common causes: acute gastroenteritis (vomiting and diarrhea) and poor oral intake during a febrile illness.

🧒 Why children are vulnerable
A higher body-surface-area-to-weight ratio, greater insensible losses, immature kidneys that can’t concentrate urine well, and total dependence on caregivers for fluids. Infants are about 70–80% water (children over 1 year about 65%, adults 45–60%), and children have a higher metabolic rate with lower fluid and electrolyte reserves.
Severity and signs

Severity: mild under 5% body weight loss; moderate 5–10%; severe over 10%. The most reliable indicator in an infant is change in body weight compared with a recent known weight.

Signs: weight loss, decreased tears, decreased urine output, dry mucous membranes, irritability, lethargy, sunken fontanels (infants), sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor, delayed capillary refill, cool or mottled skin, tachypnea, tachycardia. Severe: weak or absent pulses, low BP, and altered mental status as late signs of shock. A quick “at-the-door” check for severe dehydration is decreased level of consciousness, breathing difficulty (tachypnea), and mottled skin with delayed capillary refill. After 24 hours of diarrhea, expect a concentrated urine specific gravity (1.035); 1.010–1.020 is within the expected range, and 1.005 suggests excess fluid intake.

🤮6.2 Gastroesophageal Reflux (GER) vs. GERDGI · Slides 5–6 + reading

GER is the physiologic backward flow of stomach contents into the esophagus. It’s normal in infancy: up to 50% of infants under 3 months, peaking at 4 months, resolving by 12–18 months. Babies who feed well and gain weight (“happy spitters”) need no treatment.

GERD is GER that becomes pathologic, causing troublesome symptoms: poor weight gain, irritability or pain with feeding, respiratory complications, or esophagitis.

🌀 Clinical presentation
Sandifer syndrome: arching of the back and neck extension during or after feeds, which may mimic a seizure. Also frequent spit-up, poor weight gain, irritability, and chronic cough or aspiration. Red flags include such as chronic cough, wheeze, stridor, apnea, and bilious or projectile vomiting, which need provider follow-up.
Management
1️⃣ Non-drug first-line
Smaller, more frequent feeds; upright positioning for 30 minutes after feeds. Thicken feeds with rice cereal, never honey (infant botulism). Older children and teens with nighttime symptoms can raise the head of the bed 6–8 inches with risers or a wedge under the mattress (not stacked pillows), avoid eating a few hours before bed, and keep a healthy weight.
2️⃣ Medications, 3️⃣ Surgery
H₂ blockers (famotidine) or PPIs (omeprazole) for moderate–severe GERD. Omeprazole: take before meals, often first thing in the morning; don’t chew or crush capsules; delayed-release capsules can be opened into 1 Tbsp of applesauce. Fundoplication is reserved for severe, refractory GERD.
❗ GERD key points
  • Never thicken feeds with honey in infants under 12 months (botulism).
  • Never position an infant prone or flat supine after feeds (aspiration risk). Keep upright while awake.
  • for every sleep, infants go supine (on the back) on a firm, flat, non-inclined surface. Upright positioning is an awake-time strategy only. No wedges, inclined sleepers, or positioning products for infant sleep.
  • Sandifer syndrome = arching with reflux, NOT a seizure disorder.
  • GERD is a clinical diagnosis; pH probe or endoscopy only if uncertain.
🫒6.3 Pyloric StenosisGI · Slides 7–8 + reading

Pyloric stenosis is the most common surgical condition of the infant stomach: the pyloric muscle progressively thickens and narrows the stomach’s outlet.

👶 Clinical presentation
Onset at 2–8 weeks of life, NOT present at birth. Non-bilious projectile vomiting immediately after every feed; the infant remains hungry after vomiting (the classic distinguishing feature). Olive-shaped RUQ mass; visible peristaltic waves. Males 4:1.
🧪 Metabolic consequences
Repeated loss of stomach acid (HCl) → hypochloremic, hypokalemic METABOLIC ALKALOSIS: ↓ Cl, ↓ K, ↑ pH, ↑ HCO₃. An ECG to monitor for dysrhythmias related to electrolyte imbalance.
Diagnosis & management
  • Ultrasound (gold standard): pyloric wall over 4 mm, channel over 14 mm. Upper GI: “string sign.” The olive mass is NOT always palpable, so ultrasound confirms.
  • Correct electrolytes FIRST: NS + KCl until pH is normal, Cl over 100, K over 3.5, then Ramstedt pyloromyotomy. Surgery before correcting the alkalosis is an anesthesia risk.
  • In a pyloromyotomy, a small incision above the navel releases the tight muscle (open or laparoscopic) and restores normal emptying. No long-term problems if caught early and stabilized.
📚 Sources differ: feeding after surgery
📘 Slide 8: small frequent feeds resume 4–6 hours post-op; some vomiting is expected. 📗 Reading: babies can typically begin clear liquids within 24 hours, and mild vomiting for several days is expected (swelling at the surgical site).
❗ Key points
Non-bilious projectile vomiting in a 2–8-week-old = pyloric stenosis until proven otherwise. Never proceed to surgery without correcting metabolic alkalosis. Bilious vomiting at any age = a different diagnosis (think malrotation, 6.8).
👄6.4 Cleft Lip & Cleft PalateGI · Slides 17–18 + reading

Congenital craniofacial defects from incomplete fusion of facial structures during fetal development: cleft lip = failure of the maxillary and medial nasal processes to fuse (5–7 weeks’ gestation); cleft palate = failure of the palatal shelves to fuse (7–12 weeks). Multifactorial: genetic predisposition plus environment (maternal smoking, alcohol, certain medications, folate deficiency, diabetes).

👀 Clinical presentation
Visible opening in the lip and/or palate · feeding difficulty and poor weight gain · nasal regurgitation during feeding · difficulty creating suction · recurrent otitis media and conductive hearing loss (especially cleft palate) · delayed speech and language · dental abnormalities. A palate cleft can be seen or felt with a gloved finger, and may go undetected for a while.
🔎 Nursing assessment
Airway and respiratory status · feeding ability and nutrition · weight gain and hydration · signs of aspiration · oral cavity · family coping and bonding · hearing and speech development.
Nursing interventions
  • Specialized bottles (Haberman, Pigeon, Dr. Brown’s specialty bottles).
  • Feed upright (60–90°).
  • Burp frequently.
  • Monitor weight closely.
  • Parent education and emotional support.
🛠️ Surgery & the team
Cleft lip repair (cheiloplasty) within the first few months after birth; cleft palate repair (palatoplasty) around 9–12 months. Surgery closes the cleft, improves feeding, aids speech, and prevents complications. After surgery, feeding techniques and positioning minimize pressure on the wound (spoon, syringe, or specialized nipples), balanced against keeping nutrition up. The team includes feeding specialists and lactation consultants, ENT (ear and eustachian tube problems), dental, orthodontics (re-aligning teeth), and speech therapy (can start in infancy).
🔥6.5 AppendicitisGI · Slides 9–10 + reading

Appendicitis is the most common surgical emergency in children. Something blocks the opening of the appendix (a fecalith or lymphoid hyperplasia) → bacterial overgrowth → ischemia → perforation if untreated. Perforation spills infected contents into the abdomen, causing peritonitis (which can be life-threatening, with sepsis); highest risk at ages 10 to 19, males, family history.

📍 Clinical presentation
Pain begins periumbilical and migrates to the RLQ at McBurney’s point. Rebound tenderness (worse on release); Rovsing’s sign (LLQ pressure → RLQ pain). ↑ WBC, ↑ CRP.
💥 Perforation warning
SUDDEN RELIEF of pain followed by generalized worsening = possible perforation; board-like rigid abdomen, high fever.
Diagnosis & management
  • Abdominal ultrasound preferred (no radiation). CT or MRI may also be used; a high WBC supports but isn’t required for diagnosis; obtain a pregnancy test in adolescents who could be pregnant.
  • NPO immediately, IV fluids, pain management, antibiotics before surgery.
  • Laparoscopic appendectomy is the gold standard.
  • Perforated: IV antibiotics for 7–14 days; monitor for abscess and wound infection.
  • Before surgery, keep the child in a position of comfort with the head elevated (semi-Fowler’s as tolerated).
❗ Appendicitis key points
Sudden relief of RLQ pain = possible perforation, so notify the provider IMMEDIATELY. NEVER apply heat to the RLQ (increases perforation risk). Rebound tenderness = pain worse on release (peritoneal irritation). Rovsing’s sign is distinct from rebound tenderness.
🎈6.6 Hirschsprung DiseaseGI · Slides 11–12

Hirschsprung disease is a congenital absence of ganglion cells (nerve cells) in the distal colon, causing a functional obstruction; the bowel above it dilates into a megacolon. Associated with Down syndrome.

👶 Clinical presentation
FAILURE TO PASS MECONIUM within 48 hours of birth: the most classic early sign. Abdominal distension, bilious vomiting. Explosive stool and gas after a rectal exam (“squirt sign”).
☠️ HAEC
Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis: fever, explosive foul diarrhea, distension, septic appearance. Life-threatening. Treatment: IV fluids, IV antibiotics, rectal decompression.
Diagnosis & management
  • Rectal suction biopsy (gold standard): no ganglion cells.
  • Contrast enema shows a transition zone (narrow aganglionic bowel → dilated proximal bowel), but doesn’t confirm on its own.
  • While waiting for surgery: rectal irrigations / decompression.
  • Definitive treatment: transanal pull-through (Swenson, Duhamel, or Soave).
❗ Key points
No meconium over 48 hours + distension + explosive stool after rectal exam = Hirschsprung. Rectal suction biopsy is the gold standard, not a contrast enema alone. Fever + explosive foul diarrhea + distension in known Hirschsprung = suspect HAEC, notify the provider.
🔭6.7 IntussusceptionGI · Slides 13–15 + reading

Intussusception occurs when one bowel segment telescopes into an adjacent segment (most often ileum into cecum), compressing the mesentery and causing ischemia if untreated. As it worsens, blood and mucus slough into the stool; untreated, it can cause obstruction, strangulation, necrosis, perforation, shock, and peritonitis. About 90% of cases have no known cause; recent viral or bacterial infection, enlarged intestinal lymph nodes, polyps, and bowel anomalies raise risk. Males are 3 times more likely to be affected.

🟢6.8 Bilious Vomiting in the Newborn: Malrotation/Volvulus, Duodenal Atresia & Meconium IleusGI · Slides 19–23
🟢 Bilious emesis in a neonate is NEVER normal
Bile enters the duodenum through the ampulla of Vater, so bilious vomiting means the obstruction is at or below the duodenum. Rule out first: malrotation with midgut volvulus, which can cause bowel necrosis within hours. Bilious emesis = NPO + notify the provider immediately + prepare for imaging. Bloody stools + abdominal rigidity = bowel necrosis = surgical emergency. Do NOT feed, treat it as ordinary spit-up, or delay reporting. The major causes include malrotation/volvulus, duodenal atresia, meconium ileus, and Hirschsprung disease.
Malrotation with midgut volvulus

Malrotation leaves the midgut mesentery on an abnormally narrow pedicle. The unfixed midgut can twist (volvulus) around the SMA, blocking the lumen AND cutting off blood supply, with necrosis within hours if untreated.

  • Presentation: sudden-onset bilious vomiting in the first week of life, abdominal pain, irritability. Bloody stools are a LATE sign of ischemia; peritonitis is a late, surgical-emergency finding.
  • Diagnosis: Upper GI series (gold standard): “corkscrew” sign. Ultrasound with Doppler: “whirlpool” sign.
  • Management: emergency surgical consult; NPO, IV fluids, NG decompression. Ladd’s procedure: divide Ladd’s bands, widen the mesenteric base, derotate the volvulus, appendectomy.
Duodenal atresia
Congenital closure of the duodenal lumen from failure to recanalize (weeks 8–10 of gestation). Strongly associated with Down syndrome (up to ~30%).
Presentation: bilious or non-bilious vomiting (depends on position relative to the ampulla) beginning hours after the first feed; minimal distension (the obstruction is high); history of polyhydramnios.
Diagnosis: abdominal x-ray “double bubble” (two gas bubbles, no distal gas); karyotype testing.
Management: NPO, NG decompression, IV fluids pre-op → duodenoduodenostomy. Not vascular-emergent like volvulus, but still needs timely correction.
Meconium ileus
Obstruction of the terminal ileum by abnormally thick meconium. Strongly associated with cystic fibrosis; often the earliest sign of CF.
Presentation: bilious vomiting; abdominal distension often present at birth; failure to pass meconium in 24–48 hours; palpable doughy or rope-like bowel loops.
Diagnosis: abdominal x-ray “soap bubble” / ground-glass look, usually RLQ. Sweat chloride test and/or CFTR genetic testing confirm CF.
Management: uncomplicated = therapeutic Gastrografin enema (diagnostic AND therapeutic); complicated or failed = surgery. Coordinate the CF team, genetics, nutrition, and pulmonology.

🧭6.9 Endocrine Basics & Type 1 DiabetesEndo · Slides 3–4 + reading

Endocrine basics: hormones are chemical messengers controlled by feedback loops at the hypothalamus, pituitary, and target organs. The anterior pituitary releases GH, ACTH, TSH, LH, FSH, and prolactin; the posterior pituitary stores and releases ADH and oxytocin. TSH works by negative feedback: too little thyroid hormone → the pituitary releases more TSH. The pancreas’s islets of Langerhans make insulin (lowers glucose) and glucagon (raises it). A newborn screen checks for about 26 metabolic disorders.

Type 1 diabetes mellitus

Autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells → absolute insulin deficiency. Hyperglycemia develops once about 80–90% of β-cells are destroyed; usually linked to HLA genes plus an environmental trigger such as a viral illness. Type 1 is the most common pediatric diabetes.

👀 Clinical presentation
Classic “3 Ps”: polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia. Also weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, nocturnal enuresis, fruity breath, and Kussmaul respirations (late). Hyperglycemia can also cause dehydration and altered mental status.
🧒 Pediatric vs. Adult
Children have an abrupt onset and are far more likely to present in DKA at diagnosis; because growth is still active, it can be affected. Earlier onset (under 7) is linked to slightly lower cognitive function, memory, and attention.

Diagnostics: blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL with symptoms; A1C ≥ 6.5%. Autoantibodies (GAD65, IA-2, insulin antibodies) and a low C-peptide support the diagnosis. Low C-peptide + positive autoantibodies distinguish type 1 from type 2.

Treatment

Basal-bolus insulin, continuous glucose monitoring, insulin pump therapy, carbohydrate counting, and exercise planning. Basal insulin once or twice daily; bolus insulin at meals based on carbs and current glucose.

📌 General nursing knowledge (not in your course files)

  • Sick days: check glucose and ketones every 2–4 hours; call the provider for moderate–large ketones, vomiting, or trouble breathing.
  • Injection sites: rotate them to prevent lipohypertrophy.

Assess glucose trends, injection sites, growth, weight, hydration, and ketones. ⚖️6.10 Type 2 DiabetesEndo · Slides 5–6 + reading

Type 2 diabetes begins with insulin resistance and hyperinsulinemia (the body makes insulin, but cells don’t respond well, so the pancreas overproduces), progressing to β-cell dysfunction and eventual insulin deficiency.

👀 Clinical presentation
Obesity, acanthosis nigricans, polyuria, fatigue, hypertension, and dyslipidemia.
🧒 Pediatric vs. Adult
Children show more rapid progression, more severe insulin resistance, a strong family history, and earlier complications than adults.

Diagnostics: A1C, fasting glucose, oral glucose tolerance test, lipid panel, liver enzymes.

Treatment: lifestyle changes (nutrition, exercise) are first-line; medications include metformin, GLP-1 agonists in selected patients, and insulin if severe. Metformin: follow the exercise and diet guidance; tell the provider about other medications; a missed dose can be taken late, but never double a dose.

❗ Type 2 key points
Acanthosis nigricans + obesity in a child = classic clue for type 2. Pediatric type 2 progresses faster and complicates earlier than adult-onset disease. Family-centered lifestyle change is the foundation of treatment, not medication alone. Nursing: weight management, lifestyle counseling, family-centered education, and monitoring adherence.
🧠6.11 Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA)Endo · Slides 7–8 + reading

DKA is different in children: it carries the highest risk of cerebral edema and is the leading cause of diabetes-related death in children. DKA is most associated with type 1 but can occur in type 2.

⛓️ Pathophysiology
Insulin deficiency → hyperglycemia → lipolysis → ketone production → metabolic acidosis → osmotic diuresis → severe dehydration.
👀 Clinical presentation
Early: polyuria, vomiting, abdominal pain. Late: Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, lethargy, altered LOC.
🧠 Signs of cerebral edema
Headache, bradycardia, hypertension, declining LOC, cranial nerve palsies.
Diagnostics
Glucose over 200Venous pH under 7.30Bicarbonate under 15Positive ketonesElectrolytesABG/VBG

  • NO insulin bolus. NO bicarbonate unless acidosis is extreme.

Hourly neuro checks, strict I&O, cardiac monitoring, monitor potassium, assess for cerebral edema, family support.

🦋6.12 Hypothyroidism & Hyperthyroidism (Graves Disease)Endo · Slides 9–11 + reading

Thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) regulate metabolism, heart rate, and body temperature, and in young children they’re essential for brain growth, neurodevelopment, and myelination.

Hypothyroidism

May be congenital (thyroid dysgenesis, dyshormonogenesis) or acquired (Hashimoto thyroiditis).

👶 Infants
large tongue, hypotonia, constipation, prolonged jaundice. Elevated birth weight, delayed stooling, poor feeding, noisy respirations, hoarse cry, jaundice.
🧒 Children
short stature, cold intolerance, dry skin, delayed puberty. Weight gain, goiter, slow growth, delayed bone maturation, dry skin, sleep apnea. Both types: constipation, lethargy, cold intolerance.

Pediatric vs. Adult: children show growth delay, developmental delay, and poor school performance; adults show fatigue, weight gain, and constipation. Diagnostics: TSH, free T4, thyroid antibodies, newborn screening. TSH is the most accurate test; T4 and TSH are checked monthly until T4 normalizes, then every 3 months until age 3, then every 6 months.

💊 Levothyroxine: lifelong replacement
For congenital hypothyroidism, start treatment before 2 weeks of age if possible. Tablets can be crushed and mixed in 1–2 tsp of water, given right away (don’t store). Don’t mix with formula or food, especially foods with calcium, iron, zinc, or magnesium, which block absorption. A liquid form also exists. Adverse effects (signs of too much) include heat intolerance, hyperactivity, insomnia, irritability, tremor, sweating, and weight loss.

Nursing care: medication education, monitor growth and developmental milestones, and teach the need for lifelong therapy.

❗ Hypothyroid key points
Newborn screening allows early identification of congenital hypothyroidism, preventing severe developmental impairment. Growth delay and poor school performance are pediatric-specific findings, not seen once growth is complete. Levothyroxine is lifelong, so adherence teaching is a core nursing responsibility.
Hyperthyroidism: Graves disease

Graves disease is the most common cause of pediatric hyperthyroidism: autoimmune overproduction of thyroid hormone. More common in females.

👀 Presentation
weight loss, tachycardia, heat intolerance, goiter, exophthalmos, hyperactivity; hyperactivity and behavior change are especially prominent in children. Irritability, feeding difficulty, hypertension, tachycardia, exophthalmos, goiter, failure to thrive, vomiting, diarrhea. Hand tremors and increased sweating are also findings.
🧪 Diagnosis & treatment
Low TSH, high T4, positive TSI antibodies. Methimazole (the preferred antithyroid drug in children) and beta-blockers (propranolol) for symptoms. Surgery and radioactive iodine are used less often in children. Methimazole adverse effects include rash and agranulocytosis/low blood counts and bleeding; take a missed dose as soon as possible; monitor weight. Propranolol: bradycardia, hypotension, cold extremities.
⚠️6.13 Congenital Adrenal Hyperplasia (CAH)Endo · Slide 12

CAH is usually caused by 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Follow the chain: low cortisol → high ACTH → adrenal hyperplasia → excess androgen production → salt wasting in the classic form. Medical emergency in newborns. Adults are usually diagnosed earlier in life and don’t present acutely this way.

👀 Clinical presentation
Girls: ambiguous genitalia. Boys: normal genitalia, so it may be missed longer. Salt-wasting crisis: vomiting, shock, poor feeding, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia.
🧪 Diagnostics
17-hydroxyprogesterone (elevated), electrolytes, ACTH level, genetic testing.

Treatment: hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone replacement, salt supplementation, and stress-dose steroids during illness, surgery, or injury. Nursing care: monitor electrolytes, prevent adrenal crisis, teach emergency steroid administration.

💎 Clinical pearl
Newborn screening allows early identification of classic CAH, preventing adrenal crisis.
📏6.14 Growth Hormone DisordersEndo · Slide 13 + reading

Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) presents with short stature, delayed bone age, poor growth velocity, and normal intelligence. GHD can be congenital or acquired (brain injury, brain surgery, CNS tumors, chemotherapy, radiation). Risk is higher with a family history, childhood cancer, and newborn midline defects (cleft lip or palate). GHD is the most common pituitary hormone deficiency.

👀 What you’d see
Body proportions and weight within expected ranges, but shorter than expected, with a drop in height of two or more percentiles on the growth chart. Decreased height velocity over time is the most critical clinical indicator. Psychosocial effects: lower self-esteem, body image issues, anxiety, depression.
🧪 Diagnostics
Bone age (hand and wrist x-ray), IGF-1 (and IGFBP-3), GH stimulation testing, and MRI of the pituitary. Random GH levels are unreliable because GH is released in pulses; in a stimulation test, medications that should trigger GH release are given, and a low response suggests deficiency.
💉 Treatment: daily GH injections
Daily subcutaneous injections of synthetic GH, given at bedtime (GH is released most during sleep). Monitor height every 3 to 6 months and bone age by x-ray. Typical growth in the first year is about 4 inches. Continue until the growth plates close. Common side effects: muscle or joint pain and swelling of hands and feet. Report right away: severe headaches, vision problems, and hip issues.

Growth hormone excess: rare, usually from a pituitary tumor; produces gigantism, large hands and feet, and rapid growth.

❗ GH key points
Poor linear growth velocity, not just short stature alone, is the hallmark of GH deficiency. Normal intelligence distinguishes GHD from many other causes of developmental delay. At a follow-up visit, the priority assessment is growth trends.

🌸6.16 Precocious (and Delayed) PubertyEndo · Slides 16–18 + reading

Precocious puberty is pubertal onset before age 8 in girls or before age 9 in boys. Pathophysiology: central = early activation of the HPG axis; peripheral = hormone production outside the HPG axis. More common in females; rapid early growth can end early, leading to short adult stature.

📚 Sources differ: the age cutoff for girls
📘 Slide 16: before 8 in girls, before 9 in boys (“know the cutoffs precisely”). 📗 Reading: current guidelines consider it precocious only when breast development or pubic hair appears before 7 in non-Hispanic White girls and before 6 in African American girls, while noting the traditional definition is before 8 (girls) and 9 (boys).
👀 Clinical presentation
Breast development, testicular enlargement, growth spurt, advanced bone age, acne, body odor. In girls, puberty starts with breast buds, then pubic and axillary hair and body odor; menarche about 2 years after breast buds. In boys, the first sign is testicular enlargement (subtle and easy to miss); penis and scrotum growth follow about 1 year later.
🧪 Diagnostics & treatment
Bone age, LH/FSH (good screening tests; above range suggests precocious, below range suggests delayed), GnRH stimulation test, brain MRI, pelvic ultrasound. Central: GnRH agonists. Peripheral: treat the underlying cause. Leuprolide: monitor response with a stimulation test, LH, or sex steroid levels; side effects include headache, hot flashes, sweating; report emotional lability (crying, irritability, anger, aggression).
❗ Key points
Girls before 8, boys before 9. Psychosocial support matters as much as medical treatment: early puberty is emotionally difficult for a child.
Delayed puberty

Consider it for a girl who is at least 13 with no breast development, has more than 4 years between breast buds and menarche, or is 16 or older without menarche; and for a boy with no testicular enlargement by 14. Risk is higher with chronic illness, nutritional deficiency, or congenital anomalies. A 12-year-old girl with no breast buds is still within the expected range.

🦠A.1 Pneumonia & InfluenzaUnit 4 Mod 3
Pneumonia
Children have fewer cough receptors than adults, so it takes more secretions to trigger a cough. Infants and young children with just fever and cough should be assessed for pneumonia, and parents may first notice faster breathing and more effort. Neonates may feed poorly and be fussy and hard to console. Viral pneumonia comes on gradually after a URI, with wheezing as the main lung sound. Atypical (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia) starts abruptly with headache, fever, conjunctivitis, light sensitivity, sore throat, and malaise before the cough. Bacterial pneumonia needs immediate empiric antibiotics; viral pneumonia doesn’t need antibiotics (influenza pneumonia can be treated with oseltamivir, started without waiting for lab confirmation). Prevention: Hib and pneumococcal vaccines, plus the yearly flu vaccine.
Influenza
Types A and B (A is usually more severe). Seen most in late autumn, winter, and early spring. Signs: fever, malaise, headache, muscle aches, rhinitis; usually gone within 7 days. Diagnosed by nasopharyngeal swab (rapid test; PCR is most accurate). Highest risk: under 5 (especially under 2), under 6 months, and children with underlying conditions. Complications: secondary bacterial infection (pneumonia is most common), sepsis, febrile seizures, encephalitis, Reye syndrome, Guillain-Barré, myositis (calf pain), myocarditis. The nurse educates and advocates for the yearly flu vaccine.

🧈A.3 Dyslipidemia & Infective EndocarditisUnit 4 Mod 4
Dyslipidemia
An imbalance of HDL, LDL, triglycerides, and cholesterol that raises cardiovascular disease risk. Screening for all children 9 to 11 years. Reference values for children: total cholesterol 120–200 mg/dL, LDL under 110 mg/dL. Treatment depends on age and risk: lifestyle changes (heart-healthy diet, weight loss), supplements, and medications such as statins (atorvastatin), cholestyramine, ezetimibe, and fenofibrate. Infective endocarditis
Most common organisms: Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pyogenes. Risk factors: congenital heart disease (50–70% of cases), central venous catheters, rheumatic heart disease. Most common under 1 year. Treatment: antimicrobials for 2–6 weeks (cards include vancomycin, gentamicin, oxacillin). Prevention: oral hygiene, dental visits at least every 6 months, and prophylactic antibiotics before invasive procedures for high-risk clients.

DiarrheaThree or more loose or liquid stools in 24 hours. Acute: a pathogen inflames the GI lining, reducing absorption and speeding peristalsis. 🫃A.6 Hernias, GI Bleeding & Eating DisordersUnit 4 Mod 5
Hernias
An organ or tissue pushes through a weak spot in the abdominal wall. Inguinal (most common): an opening in the groin that should have closed before birth stays open. Umbilical: the umbilical ring is slow to close; a painless bulge that gets smaller when lying supine. If the bowel becomes trapped (incarceration or strangulation), its blood supply can be cut off, and tissue can die quickly. That’s an emergency.
Causes of GI bleeding
Necrotizing enterocolitis, malrotation with volvulus, intussusception, Meckel diverticulum, esophageal varices, inflammatory bowel disease, cow’s milk protein allergy, colitis, liver disease, portal hypertension.
Eating disorders
Anorexia nervosa: restricted calories, low body weight, distorted body image, excessive exercise, fatigue, dental problems. Bulimia nervosa: self-induced vomiting, misuse of diuretics or laxatives, persistent throat discomfort, eroded tooth enamel, enlarged salivary glands, digestive issues, profound dehydration, intense exercise. Binge-eating disorder: binge episodes, eating when not hungry, eating in secret, shame or guilt. Other types include ARFID (no fear of weight gain; may fear choking on food), atypical anorexia, and purging disorder. Screening includes a CBC and iron levels at sick visits, yearly check-ups, and sports physicals.
🧠A.7 Hypopituitarism & HyperpituitarismUnit 4 Mod 6

Pituitary disorders disrupt hormone production and can affect mood, behavior, and the body. A pituitary tumor may cause weight changes, vision changes (pressure on the optic chiasm), growth changes, or mood changes. Causes include genetic conditions, head trauma, infection (such as meningitis), stroke, brain cancer, and cancer treatment. Neonates with hypopituitarism may show micropenis, hypoglycemia (lethargy, jitteriness, pallor, cyanosis), or jaundice, which can be mistaken for temperature problems or infection. An ACTH stimulation test paired with a cortisol test checks the adrenal response. Hyperpituitarism (often a tumor) causes too much of a hormone; excess GH causes gigantism.

🦋 Thyroid: “Hypo is slow, hyper is fast”

Module 6 · endocrine

Hypo: big tongue, floppy, constipated, jaundiced, cold, slow growth → levothyroxine for life, crushed in 1–2 tsp water, never formula. Hyper (Graves): fast heart, hot, thin, bulging eyes, hyperactive → methimazole (low TSH, high T4).

💦 SIADH vs. DI: Soaked vs. Dry

Module 6 · endocrine

SIADH = Soaked Inside: too much ADH, keeps water, sodium low → restrict fluids. DI = Dry Inside: too little ADH effect, loses water, sodium high → DDAVP (central). Infants with DI: poor feeding, irritability, FTT.

🧂 CAH: “Salt out, potassium up, androgens up”

Module 6 · endocrine

21-hydroxylase missing → low cortisol → high ACTH → excess androgens (ambiguous genitalia in girls) and salt wasting (low Na, high K, vomiting, shock). Hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone + salt; stress doses for illness, surgery, or injury.

🌸 Puberty cutoffs: “Girls 8, boys 9”

Module 6 · endocrine

Precocious = before 8 (girls) / 9 (boys) (newer cutoffs of 7 and 6 for some girls) → GnRH agonist (leuprolide). Delayed = no breasts by 13, no period by 16, no testicular growth by 14.

📏 GH deficiency: “Slow, not just short”

Module 6 · endocrine

Hallmark = slow growth velocity (2+ percentiles), normal proportions, normal intelligence. Bedtime subQ injections until growth plates close. Report hip problems, severe headaches, vision problems.
